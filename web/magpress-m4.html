Design pack · manual photo-magnet press

MagPress M4

A hand-lever toggle press with a sliding two-station die table. It makes square, rectangular and round photo fridge magnets in two strokes. One universal die stays on the press, and each of the eight sizes is a small hardened size kit that changes by hand in about a minute. This page has the 3D model, the force analysis, die and frame dimensions, a parts list, the manufacturing route, assembly steps, test plan and a failure review (section 11) that drove the Rev B changes.

How it makes a magnet

Each magnet takes two strokes of the handle. The die table slides between two stations. Each lower die is loaded while it is outside the press, so your fingers are never under the ram. The upper die stays on the ram and does both jobs. This is the slide-type layout from the photo you sent. The two-stroke die action follows the badge-press patents listed in section 13, all expired.

    How the folds are made

    A section through one edge of the dies, at true size and about 25 times enlarged, through both strokes. The station A parts are the pickup die, the station B parts the crimp die. Step through it or press Play.

      At the corners

      Rev E makes sharp corners, like a bought photo magnet. Along each straight edge the tin, photo and film fold down the side, then inward along the back and back on themselves: two flat layers. The tin blank and the paper have their corners clipped at 45°, so at each corner the two folded edges meet in a neat diagonal mitre on the back instead of a lump. At the very corner, where the side walls meet, about two layers of paper overlap in a small gusset: the upper ring and ring B have a Ø1.2 relief in each corner to make room for it, so it isn't crushed or torn. Round magnets keep the Rev D rolled edge, with nothing showing on the back. If the corners tear or wrinkle onto the face, the photo paper is too thick: use 90–120 g/m² paper, not card or photo paper.

      Layer stack, front to back

      Mylar film (0.075 mm PET) → printed photo (90–120 g/m² plain or inkjet matte paper; not card or photo paper) → tinplate (0.20 mm soft, a flat blank with clipped corners that stroke 1 folds into a shell; round kits use a drawn round cup) → back plate (1.2 mm ABS/acrylic, or cardboard) → adhesive magnet sheet, applied after pressing inside the folded border.

      Sellers ship slightly different stacks (cardboard backs, acrylic backs, tin backs). The die set and the consumables must match, so buy consumables to this pack's sizes or adjust the die values in section 05.

      Why these choices
      • Toggle, not cam: force rises as the stroke closes, which is where crimping needs it. The stroke ends 8° before dead centre, so it can't lock over.
      • H-frame with two columns: it is symmetric, so the ram can't tilt under load the way a C-frame does.
      • Slide, not turntable: it is simpler to make. Stop blocks set each station, and a hardened pilot pin on the ram locks the table on station before the dies meet (Rev B).
      • Grip lands over the base tongue: the press can't tip toward you at the end of the stroke.
      • Sharp corners, printed back border (Rev E): the edge folds twice, flat, under the back plate, as on bought magnets, so the photo, a date or a name can run round onto the back.
      • One die, many sizes (Rev C, round in Rev D): the springs, carriers and die body stay on the press. Only six small hardened parts change per size, and a new size up to 90 × 65, or up to about Ø65 round, is just a new kit.
      • Keyed size rings: each lower carrier has its own key pins, so a pickup ring can't go into the crimp station or the other way round.
      • One-piece lever, dry bushings (Rev B): there is no threaded handle joint to strip, and no greased pins to dry out or collect shell chips.

      04

      Force through the stroke

      The handle drives a toggle. The crank (O→A, 40 mm) and the link (A→B, 60 mm) straighten as the handle comes down. Ram movement per degree of handle travel shrinks toward the bottom, so the force multiplies, reaching about ×35 at the end. Mechanical advantage is MA = L / (dy/dφ) with dy/dφ = a·sinφ + a²·sinφ·cosφ / √(b² − a²sin²φ), where L = 320 mm (pivot to grip centre; Rev A had 290), a = 40, b = 60, and φ is the crank angle from straight down. The chart assumes 200 N on the grip and 90% efficiency through the three pins.

      The required force for each die set is an estimate: about 7 N per mm of magnet edge for the crimp spring preload, scaled from the round-badge spring figures in US 4,696,086, then × 2.2 for the final crimp. Measure it on the prototype with a load cell (section 10). Stroke 1 stops at about 34.2 mm, when the plug meets the shell; since Rev E it also folds the tin blank. The estimate includes the upper die springs, about 1.3 kN at the end of stroke 2.

      05

      One die, eight size kits

      Rev C splits the die into two groups. The universal die is made once and stays on the press: the upper die body, the spring-loaded upper carrier, two lower carriers, two station bases and all the springs. A size kit holds only the six hardened parts that touch the shell: the plug, the upper ring, the pickup ring and pedestal, and the crimp ring and anvil. Every kit ring has the same 106 × 81 outside, so one carrier pocket takes every size, and only the openings change. Rev D adds four round kits (Ø44, Ø50, Ø56, Ø58): the rings get a round bore, the plug, pedestal and anvil are turned, and the round plug hangs on a second, inner dowel pattern in the die body, as the square plug's dowels sit outside a Ø44 plug. Rev E sharpens every kit to R1 corners, folds the tin from a flat blank in stroke 1 (the pickup pedestal is 1.2 mm higher so the whole 6 mm flange is drawn into the upper ring), and changes the crimp anvil to a double flat fold: a cove at its edge turns the skirt inward along a floor 0.85 below the back-plate platform, and the platform's wall turns it up and back outward under the back plate. Round kits keep the Rev D roll channel: a round edge cannot be folded flat.

      How a size change works (about a minute, no tools)
      • Upper: loosen two thumb screws and the upper ring drops out from below. Pull the detent pin out to the left and the plug comes off its two dowels. The new plug only fits one way: square plugs on the outer dowel holes, round plugs on the inner ones.
      • Lower: screw the T-handle puller into the pedestal or anvil and lift it out (drop-in fit, F7). Then lift the ring out by its opening. Drop in the new ring, then the new pedestal or anvil on its spigot.
      • Keys: pins in each carrier match slots in its rings. A pickup ring won't seat in the crimp carrier, and a lower ring won't fit the upper carrier.
      • Depth: every kit part is made to the same heights, so the shim pack set once on the press suits every size.
      Materials and springs
      • Kit parts: A2 / 1.2363 at 58–60 HRC. Wire-EDM with two skim cuts, then re-temper to remove the brittle recast layer. Polish the forming edges to Ra 0.2 and finish with thin dense chrome. For high volume, use PM steel tempered above 500 °C with PVD TiCN or TiAlN. For the prototype kit, use 7075-T6.
      • Universal die: carriers in 42CrMo4 QT, body in 1.2312, bases in C45, all black oxide. The ring pockets are made to +0.01/0 and the rings to −0.01/−0.02; the pedestal and anvil spigots drop into F7 bores.
      • Springs (same for every size): upper, 8 × ISO 10243 Ø12.5 × 44 red (Rev E, was Ø10 × 32 blue), 1.57 kN preload rising to 2.54 kN: they hold the upper ring down while stroke 1 folds the flat tin blank, about 1.7 kN for 65 × 90. Lower, 4 × Ø10 × 32 green per carrier, 75 N rising to 0.38 kN at the stop. Replace the upper springs every 100,000 strokes and the lower ones every 250,000.

      Square and rectangular kits

      Round kits (Rev D)

      Fits are starting values. Upper ring opening = shell + 0.35 mm, so it grips the wrapped shell by friction; the plug = shell + 0.30 mm and slides in it. The crimp ring opening = shell + 0.45 mm, and the anvil block (shell + 0.43) slides in it. Lap the upper ring on the first prototype kit until a wrapped shell lifts out of station A reliably and pushes out cleanly at station B. The upper ring has a short 0.3 × 30° lead-in, so it grips over 2.5 mm of straight wall, four M3 ball plungers that stop the wrapped shell dropping out while the table slides, and a Ø1.2 relief in each corner for the folded paper.

      Why not one stepped die for every size?

      What a stepped die would need, and why the kits do it differently
      • The sizes don't stack. A step can only sit inside a bigger step. The 63.5 square is 10.5 mm taller than 53 × 80, and 53 × 80 is 16.5 mm longer than the square, so those two can't both be steps of one die.
      • Walls too thin. Even where sizes do stack (50 × 50 inside 53 × 80), the wall between two forming edges would be about 1.5 mm of hardened steel (0.75 mm for the square inside 65 × 90). That is the corner-cracking failure from section 11.
      • Steps get in the way. The edge of a big shell has to fold under its back plate, and a smaller step's ledge sits right where that edge has to go. In the plug, the joint between steps would print a line across the big magnet's photo.
      • What Rev C keeps from the idea: one die does the work for every size, and a universal ledge in each carrier supports every ring from below. Only the thin forming "steps" (the kit rings, pedestals and plug) change, each with its own exact 0.35–0.45 mm fit.

      Frame and drive parts

      One frame serves all eight size kits. Base, crown and ram carry the column holes at 210.00 mm pitch. Machine all three with the same program and datum scheme, or line-bore them together, so the ram slides freely once the column nuts are torqued.

      Machine-shop drawings: Rev E drawing set MP-000 to MP-229 (40 A3 sheets, PDF) with DXF profiles for cutting and wire-EDM. The die sheets are split into universal parts (made once per press) and size-kit parts: MP-203 to MP-212 tabulated for the four square and rectangular sizes, MP-223 to MP-229 for the four round sizes. Where this page and the drawings differ, the drawings govern.

      Assembly

      Each step has a "Show in 3D" button that opens the build view at that step

        Changing the size kit

        1. Rest the handle up on its stop and slide the table so station A is under the ram.
        2. Upper: hold the upper ring, loosen its two thumb screws and lower it out to the front. Pull the detent pin out to the left by its ring, and lower the plug off its dowels.
        3. Fit the new plug (it only fits one way) and push the pin home. Push the new upper ring up so its key slots meet the pins, and tighten the thumb screws.
        4. Lower: screw the T-handle puller into each pedestal or anvil and lift it out, then lift the ring out by its opening. Drop in the new ring A and pickup pedestal on the left, and the new ring B and crimp anvil on the right. The key pins stop a ring going into the wrong carrier.
        5. Make one slow dry stroke on each station, then two test magnets. Check the corners and the crimp before running a batch.

        11

        Failure modes and Rev B fixes

        This review looked at how hand-lever badge and magnet presses fail in use, from operator guides, vendor troubleshooting pages and user reports. It then checked Rev A at the loads a person can put on the grip: 200 N for a normal push and 500 N for someone leaning on it. Each problem below has a fix in the drawings and a test that proves it.

        Rev E adds the fixes from an independent design review of Rev D (docs/research/design-review-revD.md): a locking pin the column blocked, an overstressed crown at the cheek bolts, a handle stop that weakened the column thread, and a dozen smaller fits and screw lengths. Rows 9, 15 and 20 show the changes. Rev C adds two problems found while designing the size kits. Rev B's crimp springs (red, heavy duty) were worked to 37.5% of their free length, past the heavy-duty maximum of about 30%. And the flat crimp pedestal had no surface to turn the shell edge under the back plate. Rows 8, 20 and 21 cover these.

        What goes wrong on existing presses
        • Jams from double shells or mis-loaded parts are the most common stoppage. The usual cure is a partial "kick-out" stroke on the crimp die, not force on the handle.
        • Film slips and paper shows at the edge with laser-printed photos (toner wax), heavy paper or card, or a worn crimp lead-in.
        • Cheap die sets crack or warp after a few hundred uses, and some presses arrive with the dies already out of alignment.
        • Handles that screw into soft fittings strip, and one library review reports a mechanism that sticks in the down position (both secondary reports).
        • Over-oiling soils the finished pieces. Dirt on the slide makes the table stop short of its station.
        What the Rev A stress check found
        • Handle joint: the M12 stud between bar and hub reached ≈ 465 MPa at a 200 N push and ≈ 1,160 MPa at 500 N. It would bend or snap.
        • Handle return: the two Rev A springs gave 0.8 N·m at the closed position, where reopening needs about 3.5 N·m plus friction. The handle would stay down, which is the "sticks down" report above.
        • Pivot pin O (Ø12): 542 MPa at 8 kN and more than 1,000 MPa at a 16 kN overload.
        • Nothing locked the table on station. A table 2 mm off station lands the upper frame on the lower frame's edge.
        • Base (12 mm): 0.51 mm deflection at 16 kN; die walls of 1.75 mm next to counterbores in hardened steel.

        Maintenance schedule
        WhenTask
        Each shiftWipe the die table, rails and dies. Blow shell chips out of the frames. No oil on the dies.
        Weekly or 5,000 strokesCheck the thumb screws, the die lock screw and the handle-stop setting. Light oil on the rails only.
        25,000 strokesCheck handle play (≤ 1 mm at the grip) and the column nut torque. Look for tin pick-up on the die edges; clean it with a brass brush and re-polish.
        250,000 strokesReplace the die springs and return springs. Inspect the bushings and pins. Dye-penetrant check the die corners.
        Un-jam procedure
        1. Stop. Don't force the handle past the point where it stops.
        2. Lift the handle back to its open stop.
        3. If a shell is stuck in the upper frame, slide die B under the ram with no back plate. Make a slow partial stroke, and the plug pushes the shell out into die B.
        4. Lift stuck parts out of die B by hand, wearing gloves. Use a brass or plastic rod, never a screwdriver, near the frames.
        5. Check the frame bores and lead-ins for scratches before you restart.

        Before you sell it

        The two-stroke badge-die principle is described in patents that have now expired (US 4,696,086, US 6,038,944, US 6,938,518). This pack uses its own frame and die geometry rather than copying a particular vendor's machine. Before selling in your market, have a patent agent search for current patents and registered designs on square magnet presses, and give the product its own look and name.
